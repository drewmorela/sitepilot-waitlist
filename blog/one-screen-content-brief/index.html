The one-screen content brief: what the SEO agent needs before it drafts

Most bad drafts were bad before anyone started writing. The topic was vague, the reader was "everyone," nobody said what the post should leave out, and the only proof on hand was a guess. A writer, human or agent, fills those gaps with filler, and the founder ends up rewriting.

This post is for founders of small software companies, one to ten people, who approve posts rather than write them. The goal behind every post on this blog is the same: more qualified people engaging with the product every month while the team keeps building. A short brief is where that goal turns into one concrete post.

The rule here is simple. If the brief does not fit on one screen, it is not ready. If it does fit, the agent has everything it needs and the founder has almost nothing to edit later.

Why one screen is the right size

A long brief feels thorough, but it hides the decisions. When a brief runs to three pages, the reader, the angle, and the limits get buried under background, and the agent weights everything equally. A brief that fits on one screen forces the person writing it to choose.

It also keeps review fast. The founder can read the whole brief in under a minute, say yes or send it back, and move on. That is the same pattern behind approving without becoming the editor: decide early, in a small surface, so you are not editing late in a large one.

The brief does not replace the two standing documents the agent already uses. The one-page brand voice notes cover how every post sounds. The cluster sentence covers what the whole blog is about. The brief covers only what is specific to this one post.

The seven lines

Every brief on this blog uses the same seven lines. Each one is a sentence or a short list, never a paragraph.

LineWhat it says
ReaderOne specific person and the moment they are in
QuestionThe question they would actually type or ask, in their words
SliceWhich part of the cluster this covers, from the keyword map
PromiseWhat the reader can do after reading that they could not before
Out of scopeTwo or three things the post will not cover
EvidenceWhere the proof comes from, or "none yet, write as a method"
Next stepThe one link or action at the end

If a line cannot be filled in, that is the signal. The post is not ready to draft, and it goes back to the topic queue instead of into a rewrite loop later.

Where each line comes from

None of these lines should be invented on the spot. Each has a source the team already keeps.

  • Reader and question come from a real customer conversation, support ticket, or sales call, the way the customer-question brief describes. If nobody has asked it, it is a guess, and guesses get weak posts.
  • Slice comes from the cluster keyword map. One phrase, said once. This is also what the title tag will be built from later.
  • Promise is written by whoever owns the topic queue. It should be testable: after the post is drafted, someone can check whether the post keeps it.
  • Out of scope comes from neighboring posts. If a nearby post already covers a subtopic, name it here and link to it instead of repeating it.
  • Evidence comes from the customer-evidence snippets you have collected. If there is none, the brief says so plainly, and the post teaches a method instead of claiming a result.
  • Next step is usually the waitlist, a trial, or one related post. Pick one.

How the brief connects to the rest of the pipeline

The brief is the first gate, not the only one. It feeds the next steps in order:

  1. Brief. Seven lines, approved by the founder in under a minute.
  2. Outline. The agent turns the promise into five H2s and runs the fail-closed outline check. If an H2 does not serve the promise, it is cut before any paragraph exists.
  3. Draft. Written to the outline, in the brand voice, inside the out-of-scope fence.
  4. Quality gates. The draft runs through the checks before publishing.
  5. Title and description. Written last, from the slice and the promise. The title tag follows the cluster sentence title check.

When a draft fails late, look at the brief first. Most late failures trace back to a line that was left vague, usually the promise or the out-of-scope list.

Common ways a brief goes wrong

A brief can fit on one screen and still be weak. These are the usual problems:

  • The reader is a category. "SaaS founders" is too broad. Name the moment: a founder who just missed a publishing week, or who is about to hire a writer.
  • The question is a keyword. "Content brief template" is a search phrase, not a question. Write what the person would say out loud.
  • The promise is a topic. "Explain content briefs" is not a promise. "Write a brief in ten minutes" is.
  • Out of scope is empty. Every post has neighbors. If nothing is excluded, the draft will wander into them.
  • Evidence is made up. If there is no real proof, say "none yet." A method post with honest framing beats a post with invented numbers.

Each of these is a line the founder can point to when sending the brief back. No rewriting required.

Start this week

  1. Copy the seven-line table into your topic queue as a template.
  2. Fill it in for the next post before anyone starts drafting.
  3. Read it once. If it does not fit on one screen, cut until it does.
  4. If a line is empty, move the topic back to the queue.
  5. After the post ships, check whether it kept the promise line.
