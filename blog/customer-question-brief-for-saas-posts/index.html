The customer-question brief: a better starting point for SaaS posts

A product blog gets sharper when its starting material is a real question from a real buyer. “How do I keep our help center current?” is more useful than a vague assignment like “write something about growth.” The question carries the reader, the moment of need, and the limit of what the product should promise.

This is the brief SitePilot uses when a small software team wants useful posts without turning the founder into a full-time editor. It is not a survey of every possible keyword. It is a compact record of what someone is trying to decide, what the team can prove, and where the reader should go next.

Start with the question, not the headline

Collect questions from places where buyers already ask for help: sales calls, support threads, onboarding notes, and product feedback. Remove names and private details. Keep the wording close to how the person asked it.

Then write a one-line job statement:

A founder of a small SaaS company wants to decide whether a repeatable publishing process can fit around a shipping week.

That sentence is a better guardrail than a clever headline. It keeps the draft aimed at a decision instead of a broad topic. For the same audience, a post about a two-post week can link to the practical cadence guide, but the question determines whether that link belongs.

Use this five-line capture before outlining:

| Brief field | What to record | Example |

| --- | --- | --- |

| Question | The buyer’s plain-language question | “What should we publish when product work is full?” |

| Moment | When the question appears | The founder plans next week’s work |

| Decision | What a useful answer helps them choose | Keep, narrow, or skip a slot |

| Evidence | What the team can show honestly | A checklist, example, or product workflow |

| Next step | The most natural follow-on | Read a method or join the waitlist |

A brief that cannot fill the evidence row is a signal to pause. Do not use confident language to cover a missing example.

Add boundaries before the draft gets clever

Every post needs a small “not this” list. It protects the reader from a broad article and protects the brand from claims the product cannot support.

For the example question above, the boundaries might be:

  • This is not a promise of rankings, traffic, or revenue.
  • This is not a replacement for customer interviews or product documentation.
  • This is not a plan to publish every day.
  • This is not a list of generic prompts with the company name inserted.

Boundaries improve the draft because they force specificity. Instead of promising that a process will “transform marketing,” show the reader how to choose one question, one audience, and one useful answer. If the topic touches search, the article can explain discoverability without pretending that a publishing schedule guarantees an outcome.

A quality bar such as the fail-closed QA checklist belongs here as a constraint, not as decoration. The post should have a clear structure, readable sentences, a real internal link, and an explicit approval record before it reaches the live site.

Turn one question into a small outline

Once the job and boundaries are clear, build an outline that follows the reader’s decision:

  1. Name the situation. Show why the question appears during a normal work week.
  2. Give the smallest useful method. Offer steps that can be completed without a new department or expensive tool.
  3. Show a worked example. Use a fictionalized, non-sensitive scenario and label it as an example.
  4. State the trade-off. Explain what the method does not cover.
  5. Offer the next step. Link to a deeper guide or the product only when the intent is high.

This shape prevents a common failure: a post that answers “what is this topic?” but never helps anyone act. It also makes review faster. A founder can check the question, evidence, and boundaries before debating adjectives in the title.

Keep an evidence note beside the draft

For each substantive claim, add a short source note in the working brief. The note can point to a documented product behavior, an anonymized customer question, or a clearly labeled example. It does not need to become part of the article, but it should be available during review.

| Claim type | Safe support | Red flag |

| --- | --- | --- |

| Workflow | A reproducible checklist | “Everyone does this” |

| Product behavior | A tested screen or documented step | A feature on the roadmap presented as live |

| Outcome | A modest, observable next action | Guaranteed rankings or revenue |

| Example | Fictional or anonymized scenario | Invented customer quote |

This evidence note is especially useful when a draft moves between a product expert and a writer. The writer gets enough context to be accurate; the reviewer does not have to rebuild the argument line by line.

Make the handoff reviewable in ten minutes

The final brief should let a reviewer answer five questions quickly:

  • Who is this for?
  • What question does it answer?
  • What can we prove or demonstrate?
  • What should we avoid claiming?
  • What is the next useful page?

If those answers are visible, approval becomes a decision rather than a rewrite session. SitePilot pairs that brief with a lightweight approval flow so the founder checks the argument and the boundaries, not every comma.

The result is a healthier publishing loop: questions create briefs, briefs create focused drafts, and QA decides whether a draft is ready. When no question has a defensible answer, skip the slot. A blank week is cheaper than teaching readers that the blog is noise.
