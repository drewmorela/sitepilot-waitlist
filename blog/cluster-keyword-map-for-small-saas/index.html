A one-page keyword map small SaaS teams can finish in an hour

Keyword tools tempt small software teams into a second job: catalog every phrase a stranger might type. That spreadsheet grows, shipping week arrives, and the blog stays quiet again. The founders who convert trials from organic posts do something smaller. They finish a one-page cluster map, then stop planning and draft inside it.

This is the map SitePilot builds before a cadence slot opens. It is not a ranking forecast. It is a bounded queue so a 1–10 person SaaS company can publish without drowning in research theater.

Write the cluster sentence first

One sentence. Three parts: who buys, what job they are trying to finish, and which product surface that job touches.

Examples that work:

  • “Founders of small B2B SaaS tools who need onboarding emails that raise activation without a lifecycle hire.”
  • “Ops leads at 5-person analytics products who want a weekly publish loop that survives roadmap crunch.”
  • “Technical founders selling a developer API who need buyer-facing explainers that lead to a trial, not a docs dump.”

Examples that fail:

  • “SaaS marketing tips” (no buyer, no job, no surface)
  • “Best SEO tools 2026” (listicle bait; not your niche)
  • “AI content for startups” (off-brand for a product blog that converts demos)

If you cannot write the sentence in under five minutes, you do not have a cluster yet — you have a wish. Sit with support tickets, sales notes, and the last three demo questions until the sentence is specific. The customer-question brief is a useful companion when the raw material is a real buyer question rather than a vague theme.

Pin the sentence at the top of a single markdown page or note. Everything below must serve that sentence or get cut.

Turn the sentence into 4–6 titles

From the cluster sentence, list titles a real buyer would bookmark or search when stuck. Cap at six. Eight is usually two clusters pretending to be one.

Use this shape:

| # | Title (buyer language) | Job it finishes | Natural next link |

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| 1 | Checklist for week-one onboarding emails | Ship first sequence | Product how-to or sibling guide |

| 2 | How to measure activation without vanity opens | Pick a metric | Same cluster post |

| 3 | When to use in-app vs email for the same nudge | Choose a channel | Product surface |

| 4 | Copy mistakes that stall trials | Edit before send | Waitlist / product |

| 5 | Recycle one teardown into three posts | Keep cadence | 2-post week guide |

Rules while listing:

  1. Each title must map to one decision the buyer makes this month — not a broad topic essay.
  2. Prefer verbs and constraints (“without a lifecycle hire”) over noun stacks (“onboarding best practices”).
  3. Plan one internal link per title before you draft. Orphan URLs fail the bar later; see internal links that move buyers.
  4. Kill anything you would not publish if product work is hard. Soft titles are the first to skip — remove them now.

You do not need search volume columns. If a title matches how buyers already talk on calls, it earns a slot. If it only exists because a tool ranked it “medium difficulty,” leave it off the page.

Rank titles by buyer intent (not search volume theater)

Order the six titles by how close they sit to a product engagement: trial start, demo book, upgrade question, or “how do we run this every week.”

A practical ranking:

  1. Decision posts — help the buyer choose (channel, metric, process). Highest priority.
  2. How-to / checklist posts — help them execute once. Strong for conversion when linked to the product.
  3. Ops posts — help them keep a cadence without a content hire. Important for SitePilot’s ICP; still secondary to product-decision pages.
  4. Opinion / teardown — useful once, easy to overproduce. Park at the bottom or cut.

Mark each title with intent: decide, do, ops, or opinion. Publish decide and do first inside the cluster. Use ops when you need to explain the publishing system itself (this post is one). Defer opinion unless it unlocks a clearer product path.

Reality check: a post about “10 AI writing prompts” may look popular in a volume tool and still fail brand fit for a vertical SaaS blog. Intent ranking protects the channel that already converts some readers into trials.

Hand the map to the pipeline (and stop)

When the page has a cluster sentence, 4–6 ranked titles, and a planned link each, the planning job is done. Do not open another keyword tab. Do not expand to twelve titles “just in case.”

Hand-off looks like this:

  1. Pick the next title from the ranked list for this week’s slot (default cadence: two posts per week; skip if QA fails).
  2. Lock outline — H1, ≥2 H2s, job-to-be-done, one internal link. Then draft.
  3. Run fail-closed QA before anyone sees it — originality, structure, readability, niche fit, anti-spam, links, explicit approve. The checklist lives in What QA gates should catch before you hit publish.
  4. Strike the title when it ships or when it is killed. Add a replacement only when the cluster is nearly empty.
  5. New cluster only after the current list is exhausted or clearly wrong for the product.

That stop rule is the point. Spreadsheets grow because planning feels safer than shipping. A one-page map forces the next useful article onto the calendar so more qualified people can engage with the product while the team keeps building.
