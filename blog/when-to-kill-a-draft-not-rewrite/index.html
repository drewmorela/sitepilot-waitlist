When to kill a draft instead of rewriting

Founders of small software companies burn hours polishing drafts that should have been deleted. An SEO publishing agent can make that worse by “improving” a thin outline into a longer thin outline. The fix is not more rewrite loops. It is a fail-closed kill rubric: clear signals to stop, park, or replace the draft so organic search still sends buyer-shaped readers without hiring a content team.

SitePilot’s bar matches the core promise: more qualified people engaging with their product every month while they keep building. A killed draft protects that bar. A vanity rewrite that invents proof does not.

Kill vs rewrite vs skip (three different moves)

Do not confuse these:

MoveMeaningCounts toward 2/week?Typical trigger
KillDelete or archive the draft; do not ship this idea in this formNo (nothing published)Structural fail, wrong buyer, fake claims needed to “work”
RewriteKeep the job-to-be-done; replace outline/body once with a new angleOnly if the rewrite later publishesFixable gaps: missing table, weak H2s, one bad intro
Skip slotLeave the publish day empty; keep the habit logNo publish; habit staysTime/approve missing, or kill left you without a ready draft

Skipping is a ritual — see How to skip a slot without losing the habit. Killing is a quality decision. Rewriting is a bounded repair, not an open-ended polish hobby.

The fail-closed kill rubric

Run this before a second rewrite. If two or more rows land in Kill, stop. Do not negotiate with the draft.

#SignalRewrite onceKill
1Buyer jobClear job for a SaaS founder / buyerVague “content tip” with no product decision
2Cluster fitSits on your one-page mapOrphan topic outside the active cluster
3EvidenceHonest scrap or method artifact existsNeeds invented metrics, logos, or rankings
4StructureH1 + ≥2 useful H2s + one concrete table/stepsOutline-only after a full pass
5DifferentiatorOne sentence a competitor would not copy-pasteGeneric SEO filler interchangeable with any blog
6Approve timeFounder can approve in minutesWould force the founder to become the editor
7Cadence costFits remaining open slot this weekWould require a third post or silent miss

This rubric pairs with QA gates before you publish. QA can fail a finished draft; the kill rubric should catch hopeless drafts earlier, before you burn the slot.

When a rewrite is still worth it

Rewrite once when the job is real and the failure is local:

  1. Wrong opening, right brief. Swap the intro; keep the H2 spine and the table.
  2. Missing artifact. Add the checklist, decision table, or brief fields the buyer needs.
  3. Soft claims. Delete the soft claim; replace with a method or an honest customer-evidence snippet.
  4. Link desert. Add one sibling link that answers the next question — not a footer farm.
  5. Tone drift. Align to your one-page brand voice notes (short, concrete, no fake urgency).

Hard stop after that single rewrite. A second rewrite usually means the idea is wrong, not the adjectives. Park the file, log the kill reason in one line, and pick the next title from the cluster queue.

Kill signals founders miss

These look productive and still deserve a kill:

  • “Almost a case study.” You are one invented percentage away from publish. Kill or strip to method-only.
  • “We can publish and fix later.” Fail-closed means later never comes. Kill or skip.
  • “The agent spent tokens on it.” Sunk cost is not a buyer job. Archive the draft.
  • “It’s long enough.” Word count without a decision table is still thin. Kill or rebuild from the brief.
  • “It ranks for a keyword we mapped.” Keyword fit without cluster spine becomes an orphan. Re-map or kill — see cluster keyword map.

If you catch yourself defending the draft to yourself, that is usually a kill signal.

How an SEO agent should behave

Agents (human or automated) need the same fail-closed rules founders use:

  1. Score the rubric in the draft note. Pass/fail per row — no “mostly fine.”
  2. Propose kill with a one-line reason. Example: “Kill — evidence row empty; would invent uplift.”
  3. Never auto-publish a fail. No approve → no publish (same rule as approve without becoming editor).
  4. Offer the next queued title, not a third rewrite of the dead one.
  5. Respect the cadence cap. Two QA-gated posts per week. A kill does not authorize a makeup third post the same week unless the founder explicitly expands the cap.

The agent’s job is to protect qualified engagement, not to maximize draft survival rate.

A 15-minute kill review ritual

Use this on draft day, before QA:

MinuteAction
0–3Read only the H1, description, and first paragraph. Can you name the buyer decision?
3–7Fill the seven-row rubric. Mark Kill / Rewrite / OK.
7–10If ≥2 Kill: archive file; write one-line reason; pick next title.
10–15If Rewrite: list the single change; do only that; stop.
—If OK: run qa_check.py; seek approve; publish only into an open slot.

Log kills next to skips in your Monday digest so the habit stays visible without inventing traffic.

How this protects a 2-post week

A capped cadence fails when every weak idea gets endless rewrites. Kill early so Mon/Tue (or your two slots) stay filled with defensible posts. When both slots are already shipped — as in a week that hit 2/2 by Tuesday — queue the next drafts on disk and leave them. Do not burn a third publish to look busy.

Practical sequence:

  1. Brief → draft → kill rubric → (optional one rewrite) → QA → approve → publish.
  2. On kill: skip or pull the next queued title if you still have an open slot and real capacity.
  3. On full week: park drafts with status: draft and an explicit “queued / do not ship this week” note.

Recap

  • Kill, rewrite, and skip are different moves; only publish counts toward the 2/week cap.
  • Use a seven-row fail-closed rubric before a second rewrite.
  • Rewrite once for local fixes; kill when the buyer job, evidence, or cluster fit is missing.
  • Agents propose kills with reasons; they never auto-publish fails.
  • Protect the cadence: queue for next week instead of shipping junk this week.

Dead drafts are cheap. Fake proof and endless rewrites are expensive.
