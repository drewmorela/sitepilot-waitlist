Fail-closed outline: five H2s before a single paragraph

Most bad posts were bad before the first paragraph. The angle was fuzzy, two jobs were stuffed into one title, or the middle section had nothing to say. A full draft hides that for a while. Prose is good at sounding finished. Then the founder spends twenty minutes reviewing something that should never have been written, and the kill-or-rewrite decision arrives a day late.

A fail-closed outline moves that decision upstream. Before anyone writes a paragraph, the post has to earn its structure: five H2s, each one tested. If the five do not hold up, the post does not get drafted. Nothing ships by default.

This serves the same promise as the rest of this series: more qualified people engaging with the product every month while the team keeps building. Cheap kills at the outline stage are how a small software company keeps a two-post week without the review loop eating the week.

Why five, and why H2s

Five is not magic. It is a useful forcing number for a 900 to 1,500 word post written for a founder or an early buyer.

  • Fewer than four usually means the post is one idea stretched thin. That is often a section of another post, not a post.
  • More than six usually means two posts are hiding in one. Split it, or cut until one job remains.
  • Five is enough room for a setup, two or three working sections, and a close that tells the reader what to do Monday.

H2s are the right level to test because they are what a skimming reader actually sees. They are also what the structure gate already checks for. If the H2s alone do not tell a buyer what they will be able to do after reading, the paragraphs underneath will not fix it.

The five tests each H2 has to pass

Run every heading through the same five questions. One failed test on one heading is a fix. Two or more failed headings is a kill.

TestPass looks likeFail looks like
ClaimableA plain statement a reader can agree or disagree withA topic label ("Overview", "Considerations")
On the cluster sentenceServes the same buyer and job as the cluster sentenceDrifts to a different buyer or a second job
Non-overlappingSays something no other H2 in this post saysRestates a sibling heading in new words
Answerable from what you knowYou can write it from real product work, docs, or customer questionsNeeds a statistic, quote, or case study you do not have
Earns its placeRemoving it would leave the reader stuckRemoving it changes nothing

The fourth test does the most quiet work. A heading that needs evidence you do not have is the place where invented numbers creep in later. Kill that heading now, or rewrite it around something true, such as a step you actually run or a question a customer actually asked. The customer evidence guide covers what counts.

A worked example: before and after

Here is an outline for a post an agent might propose for a small analytics SaaS. The title was "How to improve onboarding emails."

First pass (fails):

  1. Why onboarding matters
  2. Best practices for onboarding emails
  3. Tools to consider
  4. Measuring success
  5. Conclusion

Three headings are topic labels. "Tools to consider" drifts away from the job. "Measuring success" needs benchmark numbers that do not exist yet. And "Conclusion" earns nothing. That is four failed headings out of five, so this outline is a kill, not a rewrite.

Second pass, after tightening the job to one thing (passes):

  1. The first email should ask for one setup step, not a tour
  2. Day-two silence usually means the setup step was too big
  3. Split the setup step until a new user can finish it in one sitting
  4. Write the follow-up around the step they skipped, not the feature list
  5. What to check in your own signup data this week

Every heading is a claim. Each one serves one buyer (a founder whose trial users stall after signup) and one job (getting them through a first setup step). None of them needs a number the team does not have. Heading five sends the reader to their own data instead of inventing a benchmark.

The second outline took about ten minutes. The first one would have cost a full draft and a review before anyone noticed it was hollow.

Where the outline gate sits in the pipeline

The order matters more than the template.

  1. Cluster sentence is fixed for the week. It is the yardstick.
  2. Title is claimed from the topic queue only when a slot is free.
  3. Five H2s are written and nothing else. No intro, no bullets underneath.
  4. The five tests run. One fix is allowed. Two or more fails is a kill, and the title goes back to the queue or gets crossed out.
  5. Only a passing outline is drafted. The draft then runs the normal gates before anyone approves it.

Step four is the fail-closed part. A missing check is not a pass. If nobody ran the five tests, the outline did not pass. An agent that cannot answer "which test did heading three pass?" should stop and say so rather than draft anyway.

This also makes the human review shorter. When the founder opens a draft, the skeleton is already approved. They are checking voice and facts against the one-page brand voice brief, not arguing with the shape of the post. That is most of what approving without becoming the editor looks like in practice.

Common ways the gate gets skipped

  • "I'll fix the structure in the draft." You will not. Prose makes a weak structure feel solid, and the review gets longer instead.
  • Writing the intro first. A good intro makes you want to keep the outline, even when the outline is wrong. Write it last.
  • Letting sub-bullets carry the argument. If the real point lives in an H3 or a bullet, it should be the H2.
  • Keeping a dead heading because the draft is half done. Sunk words are not a reason. Cut it and check whether four headings still make a post.
  • Testing headings against the title instead of the cluster sentence. Titles move. The cluster sentence is the fixed point for the week.

Recap

  • Write five H2s before any paragraph. Fewer than four is thin, and more than six is two posts.
  • Test every heading: claimable, on the cluster sentence, non-overlapping, answerable from what you know, earns its place.
  • One failed heading is a fix. Two or more is a kill.
  • Only a passing outline gets drafted, and an untested outline counts as failed.
  • The founder reviews voice and facts, not structure.

Kill it at five headings, and you never have to kill it at 1,200 words.
